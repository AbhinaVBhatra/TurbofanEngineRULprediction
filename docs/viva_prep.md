### Q1: What is the core business problem your project addresses?
**Answer**: In aviation and industrial machinery, unscheduled engine breakdowns cause massive financial losses ($100,000 to $1M+ per event) due to emergency replacements, secondary core damage, flight groundings, and passenger compensation. Conversely, rigid calendar-based preventive maintenance overhauls healthy engines too early, discarding up to 30–40% of usable component life. Our system converts this into a data science problem: predicting **Remaining Useful Life (RUL)** to transition from reactive or rigid schedules to cost-optimal **Condition-Based Predictive Maintenance**.

### Q2: What dataset did you use, and is it real flight data?
**Answer**: We utilized the **NASA C-MAPSS** (Commercial Modular Aero-Propulsion System Simulation) dataset generated by NASA Ames and Glenn Research Centers (Saxena & Goebel, 2008). **Crucially, this is high-fidelity simulated thermodynamic data**, not commercial airline flight data. It simulates aircraft gas turbine degradation across 21 sensors and 3 flight operational settings, contaminated with realistic measurement noise.

### Q3: Why did you apply a piecewise-linear cap on RUL at 125 cycles?
**Answer**: Based on Paris' Law of crack propagation and material fatigue physics, machine components do not begin degrading on cycle 1. During early operational life, the engine operates in a healthy steady-state with zero structural degradation. If an unbounded linear target is used ($RUL = T_{max} - t$), the model is forced to fit random sensor noise during healthy cycles, creating false degradation signals. Capping the target at $RUL_{cap} = 125$ cycles (following Heimes, 2008) forces the model to predict a constant healthy plateau until true degradation begins, drastically improving accuracy near failure where decisions matter.

### Q4: How did you prevent data leakage in your preprocessing and evaluation?
**Answer**: 
1. **Engine-Grouped Splitting**: We partitioned train and validation sets strictly by unique engine ID (`unit_nr`), never by row. Random row splitting leaks time-adjacent sensor snapshots from the same engine across splits.
2. **Train-Only Fitting**: Scalers (`StandardScaler`) and clustering models (`KMeans`) were fitted strictly on the training engines partition.
3. **No Target Leakage**: The test set target labels (`RUL_FD001.txt`) were completely isolated and never used for training or tuning.

### Q5: Why did you drop sensors s_1, s_5, s_10, s_16, s_18, and s_19 in FD001?
**Answer**: In dataset FD001, the engine operates under a single, static sea-level flight condition. Consequently, ambient intake temperature ($s_1$), intake pressure ($s_5$), pressure ratio ($s_{10}$), fuel-air ratio ($s_{16}$), and demanded fan speeds ($s_{18}, s_{19}$) have zero standard deviation ($\sigma < 10^{-10}$). They carry zero prognostic information and would only add numerical noise to regression algorithms.

### Q6: Why is dataset FD004 significantly more difficult than FD001?
**Answer**: FD001 has only **1 operating condition** (sea level) and **1 failure mode** (HPC degradation). FD004 has **6 distinct flight operating regimes** (varying altitude, Mach number, and throttle angle) and **2 simultaneous failure modes** (HPC wear and Fan wear). Operating regime transitions cause raw sensor readings to jump by orders of magnitude between consecutive cycles, completely masking the gradual degradation curve unless normalized per flight condition.

### Q7: How did you normalize multi-regime datasets like FD004?
**Answer**: We implemented a `RegimeClusterNormalizer` in `src/features.py`. We fitted a $K=6$ KMeans clusterer strictly on the 3 operational settings (`setting_1, setting_2, setting_3`) of the training engines. Then, for every sensor, we computed regime-specific means $\mu_{s,c}$ and standard deviations $\sigma_{s,c}$ to normalize readings relative to their operating envelope:
$$z_{i,c} = \frac{x_i - \mu_{s,c}}{\sigma_{s,c}}$$
This eliminated operational step shifts and revealed smooth degradation trajectories.

### Q8: What feature engineering did you perform for tabular models?
**Answer**: Starting from the 14 active prognostic sensors, we engineered **99 total features** grouped strictly by engine ID:
- Raw normalized sensor readings (14)
- Rolling means across windows $W \in \{5, 15\}$ (28 features) to smooth high-frequency sensor noise.
- Rolling standard deviations across $W \in \{5, 15\}$ (28 features) to capture expanding thermodynamic turbulence/vibration near failure.
- Rolling differences/slopes across $W \in \{5, 15\}$ (28 features) to capture the rate of degradation.
- Cumulative operational cycles (`time_cycles / 100.0`, 1 feature) representing component age.

### Q9: Why is Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) alone insufficient for evaluating turbofan RUL?
**Answer**: RMSE is symmetric; it penalizes an early prediction (underestimating life by 10 cycles) identically to a late prediction (overestimating life by 10 cycles). In aviation operations, an early prediction merely causes a premature maintenance check, whereas a late prediction allows a degraded engine to continue flying, risking catastrophic in-flight shutdown, uncontained blade rupture, and loss of aircraft.

### Q10: How does the official NASA Asymmetric Scoring Function work?
**Answer**: Defined in the PHM 2008 Data Challenge:
$$d = \hat{y} - y$$
$$s_i = \begin{cases} \exp\left(-\frac{d}{13}\right) - 1 & \text{if } d < 0 \text{ (Early prediction, safe)} \\ \exp\left(\frac{d}{10}\right) - 1 & \text{if } d \ge 0 \text{ (Late prediction, hazardous)} \end{cases}$$
Because $a_2 = 10 < a_1 = 13$ and both are exponential, late predictions are penalized far more aggressively. For a 25-cycle error, a late prediction incurs a penalty of $11.18$ versus $5.85$ for an early prediction (91% higher penalty).

### Q11: What models did you compare, and which performed best?
**Answer**: We benchmarked four distinct architectures on FD001 test data (100 engines):
1. **Ridge Linear Regression**: RMSE = 19.908, NASA Score = 728.63
2. **Random Forest**: RMSE = 18.474, NASA Score = 624.83
3. **LightGBM Regressor**: RMSE = 17.160, NASA Score = 505.77
4. **PyTorch GRU (2-layer)**: RMSE = 15.223, NASA Score = 385.39

### Q12: Why did you choose LightGBM as the primary live model for web serving instead of GRU?
**Answer**: While the PyTorch GRU achieved a slightly lower RMSE (15.22 vs 17.16), LightGBM offers superior operational trade-offs for production serving:
1. Sub-millisecond CPU latency (<2 ms per engine).
2. Extremely compact model artifact (1.8 MB vs PyTorch runtime memory).
3. Zero deep-learning framework startup overhead on free hosting tiers.
4. Direct, deterministic TreeSHAP explainability.
The PyTorch GRU weights (`artifacts/FD001_gru_model.pt`) were preserved as a secondary engine.

### Q13: How did your models compare against published literature benchmarks?

### Q14: How did you implement model explainability?
**Answer**: We used **TreeSHAP** (SHapley Additive exPlanations) on the LightGBM model:
1. **Global Importance**: Aggregated the 99 engineered features into parent physical sensors. We found cumulative operating age (`cycle_norm`), Low Pressure Turbine outlet temperature ($s_4$), Low Pressure Compressor outlet temperature ($s_2$), and HPC static pressure ($s_{11}$) were the dominant physical drivers.
2. **Local Explanations**: Implemented `explain_single_engine()` which computes exact feature contributions (positive and negative) for any specific engine at request time, rendered as waterfall charts in the UI.

### Q15: How did you validate that your SHAP explanations make physical sense?
**Answer**: In FD001, the injected fault mode is **High Pressure Compressor (HPC) degradation**. When compressor blades erode, compressor efficiency drops, requiring higher fuel flow to maintain thrust. This directly causes severe thermal buildup at the HPC outlet ($s_3$) and LPT exhaust ($s_4$), as well as static pressure anomalies at $s_{11}$. TreeSHAP identified exactly these sensors ($s_4, s_2, s_{11}, s_3$) as the top degradation indicators.

### Q16: How did you quantify prediction uncertainty?
**Answer**: We trained two LightGBM models with **quantile pinball loss** at $\alpha = 0.10$ and $\alpha = 0.90$ to construct an empirical 80% prediction interval $[Q_{10}, Q_{90}]$.
We evaluated:
- **Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP)**: The percentage of true RUL values falling within the interval.
- **Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW)**: The average interval spread in cycles.
On the full trajectory validation split (3,490 cycles), empirical coverage was **89.19%** (MPIW = 54.94 cycles). On the 100 test engines at their truncated final cycles, coverage was **72.0%** (MPIW = 70.65 cycles).

### Q17: What was your maintenance decision rule?
**Answer**: We defined a condition-based decision rule: trigger an immediate depot maintenance overhaul when predicted RUL falls below a safety threshold $T$:
$$\widehat{RUL} \le T$$
We performed a threshold sweep across $T \in [10, 40]$ cycles to identify the point minimizing total fleet cost. The optimal threshold was found at **$T^* = 10\text{ cycles}$**, providing an average advance warning lead time of 10 cycles with zero catastrophic failures.

### Q18: What cost model assumptions did you use, and why?
**Answer**: We parameterized an aviation fleet cost model with clearly labeled, editable assumptions:
- $C_{\text{unplanned}} = \$200,000$: Unscheduled breakdown cost (emergency field repair, secondary engine damage, flight cancellation).
- $C_{\text{planned}} = \$40,000$: Scheduled depot shop overhaul cost (roughly 20% of unplanned).
- $C_{\text{waste}} = \$200/\text{cycle}$: Cost of discarded remaining component life ($40,000 / 200$ normal cycles).

### Q19: What were the quantified financial savings of your predictive maintenance system?
**Answer**: For a fleet of 100 turbofans simulated across their full operational lifespans:
- **Run-to-Failure Cost**: $\$20,000,000$ (100 failures $\times$ $\$200\text{k}$).
- **Optimal Fixed-Schedule Cost (130 cycles)**: $\$5,686,600$ (1 unplanned failure + 99 planned overhauls + $\$1.53\text{M}$ in wasted life).
- **Predictive Maintenance Cost ($T^* = 10\text{c}$)**: $\$4,200,000$ (0 unplanned failures + 100 planned overhauls + $\$200\text{k}$ in wasted life).
- **Net Dollar Savings**:
  - **$\$15,800,000$ saved vs Run-to-Failure (79.0% cost reduction)**.
  - **$\$1,486,600$ saved vs Fixed-Schedule Overhaul (26.14% cost reduction)**.

### Q20: What is train/serve skew, and how did your architecture prevent it?
**Answer**: Train/serve skew occurs when preprocessing logic during production inference diverges from the logic used during training (e.g. differing scaling parameters, different window calculations, or subtle order-of-operation differences).
We prevented it by enforcing a **single source of truth**: `src/features.py` and `src/inference.py`. Both offline training scripts and the live FastAPI web service import the exact same classes and functions. Our automated PyTest suite explicitly proved that offline prediction and API prediction match to within $< 10^{-4}$ cycles.

### Q21: How did you prove that the website predictions are truly computed dynamically and not hardcoded?
**Answer**: We wrote an automated unit test `test_dynamic_prediction_proof` in `tests/test_api.py`. It takes a valid sample CSV, runs the API prediction, then artificially perturbs input temperature sensors by +10%, and sends it again. The API returns a dynamically changed, lower RUL prediction, mathematically proving that inference occurs live at request time.

### Q22: What input validation does your FastAPI `/predict` endpoint perform?
**Answer**:
1. Checks that the file is a CSV or TXT file.
2. Checks for required columns (`time_cycles` and active sensor channels).
3. Verifies a minimum operational history of **at least 30 cycles** for rolling feature extraction.
4. Checks that all telemetry values are numeric, finite, and non-null.
If any check fails, the API returns a structured HTTP 422 error with human-readable error messages.

### Q23: What were the major technical bugs you hit during development, and how did you resolve them?
**Answer**: Recorded in `docs/CHANGELOG_AND_ISSUES.md`:
1. **Windows OpenMP Collision**: PyTorch CPU wheel conflicted with Anaconda's Intel MKL runtime (`OMP Error #15`). Resolved via `KMP_DUPLICATE_LIB_OK=TRUE`.
2. **NASA SSL Certificate Error**: The legacy Ames portal certificate expired under modern Python 3.13 SSL contexts. Resolved by targeting the direct NASA Open Data legacy endpoint (`https://data.nasa.gov/docs/legacy/CMAPSSData.zip`) with an unverified SSL fallback context.
3. **Pandas Rolling Index NaN Alignment**: Computing rolling features on a filtered training partition caused index misalignment, inserting 3,668 NaNs. Resolved by resetting indices before rolling operations and assigning via `.values` and `np.nan_to_num()`.

### Q24: What are the main limitations of your project?
**Answer**:
1. **Simulated Data**: C-MAPSS is simulated thermodynamic data, not real revenue airline telemetry. Real flight data has non-stationary weather, missing packets, and sensor calibration drifts.
2. **Assumed Maintenance Costs**: The \$200k, \$40k, and \$200/cycle parameters are based on literature averages; individual airline contracts vary.
3. **Batch vs Streaming**: Telemetry is processed cycle-by-cycle rather than as a continuous 100 Hz avionics data stream.
